Access is not authority

Does being able to find a recording mean you can use it for a new purpose?

A fictional situation

A community member records a language lesson for a local teaching project. Later, a copy appears in a public online archive. The recording’s permissions and community protocols are not provided in this scenario.

The situation is invented for discussion. It does not represent a particular community or its rules.

Compare a proposed use

Original teaching project

What we know: The recording was made for this project.

What remains uncertain: We do not know the agreement’s terms, its duration, or who can approve continued use.

Ask: Who should be involved in confirming that this use remains appropriate?

Compare: What changes as the purpose moves from teaching, to sharing, to AI training? What information would you need before acting?

The interface cannot decide whose permission is sufficient. Appropriate authority depends on the people, relationships, agreements, and protocols involved.

Ideas and sources behind this activity

This activity is an interpretation of the sources, not a case study reported by them.

  • Belarde-Lewis et al. (2024): Indigenous data stewardship involves relationships, trust, and authority that may be held by different community members or culture bearers. Read the article.
  • Hess and Ostrom (2007): A knowledge commons is not the same thing as unrestricted open access; governance and participation matter. Understanding Knowledge as a Commons, introduction.
  • Illingworth and Forsyth (2026): Opaque training data raises questions about representation, consent, and copyright. GenAI in Higher Education, chapter 3, pp. 38–44.
  • Lewis, editor (2020): The Indigenous Protocol and Artificial Intelligence position paper brings Indigenous and allied perspectives into the discussion of AI.
  • Heritage Lab (n.d.): Its Data Sovereignty Policy describes an Indigenous-led organisation’s approach to stewardship and community control.
